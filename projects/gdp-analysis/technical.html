Technical

How the analysis works

Collect three decades of IMF data, clean out the noise, reshape it, then read every chart against the same three crisis windows.

Collect

IMF WEO, April 2024 — 9 ASEAN economies × 12 indicators, 1980–2027.

Clean

NULL audit by year, country, indicator; drop 2025–2027 projections.

Transform

Wide-to-long reshape, helper functions, shared country color palette.

Visualize

10 core charts + 3 data-quality charts, matplotlib · seaborn · scipy.

Problem framing

Three shocks, three shapes

1998 was an idiosyncratic currency and political crisis. 2008 was a global financial shock Indonesia largely sidestepped. 2020 was a synchronized public-health shock nobody avoided. Plotting all three on one shared timeline, with crisis windows shaded, is what makes the differences legible.

Data preparation
0

projected years dropped (2025–2027)

IMF's own forward projections are excluded from every indicator so the analysis stays strictly historical — no baked-in forecast bias, and Myanmar / Timor-Leste are excluded from cross-country comparisons for data sparsity.

Three acts of crisis

Each act is read on its own terms — what triggered it, how deep it cut, and how Indonesia compared with the ASEAN-9 average.

Act I · 1997–1999
Asian Financial
Crisis
−13.1% YoY (1998)

Rupiah collapsed 85.8% against the dollar; inflation hit ~58% YoY. The deepest contraction in ASEAN, compounded by the end of Soeharto's 32-year rule.

ASEAN-9 avg: −1.9% YoY
Act II · 2008–2010
Global Financial
Crisis
+4.7% YoY (2009)

One of the few ASEAN economies to stay in positive growth, on a large domestic consumer base and disciplined monetary and fiscal policy learned from 1998.

ASEAN-9 avg: +2.1% YoY
Act III · 2020–2024
COVID-19
Pandemic
−2.1% YoY (2020)

Every ASEAN economy contracted, but Indonesia's dip was moderate next to Singapore (−4.1%) and the Philippines (−9.5%), buffered by its large domestic economy.

ASEAN-9 avg: −3.0% YoY
The SDG 8 lens

Growth alone isn't the finding

A single growth number means little without its crisis context and its regional benchmark. Every chart here pairs Indonesia's trajectory with the ASEAN-9 average and shades the three crisis windows — turning a macro time series into a story about resilience, and about where that resilience did, and didn't, translate into broader prosperity.

Nominal

USD at market exchange rates

GDP and GDP-per-capita converted at yearly-average market exchange rates. Useful for comparing economic size and cross-border flows, but sensitive to currency swings.

Purchasing-power adjusted

2021 international dollars (PPP)

Adjusts for local cost of living. Indonesia's ~$5k nominal GDP per capita climbs to ~$15k in PPP terms — narrowing, though not closing, the gap with wealthier neighbors.

Fiscal rule

A ceiling breached once

Indonesia's statutory 3% budget-deficit ceiling (UU No. 17/2003) held for 17 years, until COVID-19 forced a temporary suspension under UU No. 2/2020.
